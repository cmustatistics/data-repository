Agricultural Census for 1851, Glengarry and Vaudreuil Counties

A transcription of the Agricultural Census of Canada of 1851 for two adjacent counties 60–100 km west of Montreal between the St. Lawrence and Ottawa Rivers. Vaudreuil County in Quebec was settled under seigneurial land tenure with a predominantly French-speaking population and Glengarry County in Ontario was settled under freehold tenure, with a largely Highland Scots-Gaelic-speaking population in 1851. From the census variables, can we get a coherent picture of the variability of agricultural practices in these two counties?
Author

Kenneth Morrison Brown

Published

August 4, 2026

Data files
• GlengarryVaudreuilAgricCensus1851En.csv.gz

1852

Motivation

In our time, farms in North America are often large, highly specialized operations. But what did farming look like without herbicides, pesticides, artificial fertilizers, before mechanization, and before railways were built?

The Canadian agricultural census of 1851 collected data on the size of holding, the amount of land under cultivation, the amount of land devoted to various crops, as well as production of these crops. Data were also collected on numbers of livestock, and on the products of farm enterprise, such as wool and cloth production, dairy products, and cured beef and pork. These enterprises were partly for each family’s own needs, but there were also local markets and trade amongst neighbors. The picture that emerges is one of diversified, and by modern standards, small operations.

Is it possible to find patterns in this diversity and relate these to spatial or cultural variables, or to the size and apparent prosperity of the operation? To what extent were there very wealthy families, or poor families, and can these be detected using the variables measured? The area includes two different types of land tenure, and a diversity of cultural traditions. Do these factors have a discernible effect on farming practices?

Data

The Agricultural Censuses of Canada carried out in rural areas were meant to get an idea of the state of farming for the census year. To collect the data, trained enumerators traveled through each county to record data about each farm. The 1851 census was meant to cover farming that occurred in that year, but the enumeration was actually done in early 1852, so the accuracy of the results does depend on whether farmers kept good records.

Each row in the data represents a household and its land, and variables include the size of holding, both total and under cultivation (at this time, there was still land to be cleared), production of various crops, numbers of livestock and production from other farm enterprises (e.g., wool, cloth, butter, cheese, maple sugar). Not every row represents a farm, as there were villages, as well as rural households with livelihoods other than farming.

This extract covers Glengarry and Vaudreuil Counties, which are 60–100 km west of Montreal between the St. Lawrence and Ottawa Rivers. Vaudreuil County in Quebec was settled under seigneurial land tenure with a predominantly French-speaking population and Glengarry County in Ontario was settled under freehold tenure, with a largely Highland Scots-Gaelic-speaking population in 1851.

There are 4,632 households in the data. Data collection was by local enumerators, and there is evident variability in the way the data were collected, resulting in missing data for some variables.

Supplementary materials

The Supplementary Materials describe how the data was collected, how data cleaning was completed, how units of measure were standardized, and how the original data may be retrieved from Library and Archives Canada.

Questions

Much of the data includes measures of farm or household production, not only production from crops, but also from livestock. There are also data on investment—acres devoted to specific crops, numbers of livestock. Is it possible to create a model explaining variability in production for a specific crop or product (wool, or cloth or butter)?

Farming enterprises in this area in the nineteenth century were quite diversified, and different farms made different choices as to what enterprise to pursue. Conduct an analysis that aims to predict which households chose to include a specific crop (e.g., Barley or Rye) or product (linen or fulled cloth).

The enumerators who collected the data were generally educated and local to the area, and they were given fairly specific instructions. However, enumerators may have made different decisions about how to collect the data. As an example: where detailed farm records may not have been kept, how was production measured? How can one detect estimation in data? Are some variables more likely to be accurately measured than others? Be attentive to these issues and comment on how these might affect your analysis results.
