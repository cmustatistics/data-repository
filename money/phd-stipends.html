Ph.D. stipends and program placements

GLMs
EDA
Ph.D. students are typically paid stipends that are much lower than workers of equivalent experience could obtain in a job. Stipends vary widely between Ph.D. programs, however. Are higher stipends associated with programs producing more successful students, as measured by their placement in tenure-track jobs after graduation?
Author

Alex Reinhart

Published

November 10, 2026

Data files
• phd-stipends.csv • phd-programs.csv
Data year

2024

Motivation

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a Ph.D. student in possession of a stipend, must be in want of a raise.

Ph.D. programs vary widely between countries and disciplines, so it is hard to generalize. But in many programs, Ph.D. students are funded: the university covers the cost of their tuition and pays a modest stipend to cover the student’s living expenses. This stipend may require the student to do some work outside their academic studies, such as serving as a teaching assistant for courses, or it may pay them to conduct their thesis research. Nominally, this pay is for about 20 hours of work a week, so it is not a full-time job even though the student may spend much more than 40 hours a week on their academic work.

Whatever the case, Ph.D. stipends are paid for from university funds, research grants, fellowships, and other sources, and they are rarely generous. Stipends are almost always lower than wages for jobs in the field, and are often below what is considered a living wage. Students thus often argue for higher stipends. There are many possible arguments—based on wages for comparable jobs, on living wage standards, on the effect of low pay on mental and physical health, and so on—but one possible argument is that improved pay may improve outcomes:

This analysis looks at graduate student pay from another angle: that of program outcomes. Setting aside the debate over whether students deserve higher wages, there may be another reason for which student pay should be higher. If better wages lead to greater success, then this will be reflected in program outcomes, and higher-paying departments can reap reputational benefits as a result. (Warner and Stecuła, 2024)

This data comes from a study that aimed to answer this question. The authors gathered data on 85 Ph.D. programs in political science in the United States, including their number of graduates, program ranking, location, and outcomes: how many graduates got tenure-track jobs. Stipends are often not listed publicly, so they gathered self-reported data from PhDStipends.com, where students can report the stipends they receive or were offered by Ph.D. programs.

Data

The data is split into two files. One file contains one row per Ph.D. program, giving metadata about the program. The other file contains one row per stipend report on PhDStipends.com. Each program may have more than one reported stipend, if more than one student reported it or if students reported stipends in different years.

Variable Description
program Ph.D. program

listed Whether the program’s outcome data was available (1 = yes, 0 = no)

region US Census region of the university

Questions

  1. Determine how to merge the program and stipend information. How much do stipends vary within a program? Should you simply take the average stipend per program? Merge the data.
  2. Conduct EDA. What is the typical Ph.D. stipend? Is it related to the program’s rank, the cost of living, whether the university is public or private, or the region? Do universities with higher endowments (per student) pay better stipends? Devise plots or tables to address these questions.
  3. The core question is whether better-paying programs have better outcomes, in terms of the fraction of students who get academic jobs. Select a model to answer this question. Can you find evidence for it?
  4. The authors of the original study claim that according to their results, the stipend amount “clearly and meaningfully impacts their ability to thrive on the highly competitive job market”. That is, they regard the association as indicating that stipends cause an increase in placement. Under what conditions can we make a causal claim? Are there confounding variables, or other variables we would need to measure?
